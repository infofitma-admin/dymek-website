Thin Film Thickness Measurement: Reflectometry vs Ellipsometry vs Interferometry

Three optical methods are predominantly used for thin-film measurement. This article explains what each one measures, where it performs best and how to select the most suitable approach.

Film thickness has a direct bearing on the performance of semiconductors, optical coatings, displays, solar cells and medical devices. Most production teams measure it optically, as optical methods are both fast and non-contact. The three principal options are spectral reflectometry, spectroscopic ellipsometry and optical interferometry.

Spectral reflectometry

How it works. Light is directed onto the film at or near normal incidence. Reflections from the upper and lower surfaces of the film interfere, producing a pattern of peaks and troughs across the wavelength range. Software then fits this spectrum to a model to determine thickness and, frequently, the refractive index (n) and extinction coefficient (k).

Strengths

  • Fast, straightforward and cost-effective
  • Measures from a few nanometres up to very thick films, depending on the system
  • Readily automated for mapping and production checks
  • Small-spot versions are able to measure patterned regions

Limitations

  • Very thin films and complex stacks are more difficult to separate
  • Requires a reasonable knowledge of the materials

Typical applications. Resists, oxides, nitrides, hard coats, AR coatings, polymer films and production monitoring.

Spectroscopic ellipsometry

How it works. Light strikes the sample at an oblique angle. The instrument measures the change in polarisation on reflection, expressed as two values, Psi and Delta, across the wavelength range. A model is then used to extract thickness and optical constants.

Strengths

  • Highly sensitive to ultra-thin films, down to sub-nanometre changes
  • Measures n and k directly, including those of unknown materials
  • Well suited to complex multilayer stacks

Limitations

  • Instruments and models are more complex
  • Slower and more costly than reflectometry
  • Larger measurement spot at oblique angles

Typical applications. Gate dielectrics, ultra-thin layers, materials research and optical constant libraries.

Optical interferometry

How it works. Interferometers compare light reflected from the sample with that from a reference. They measure surface height, flatness and form. White-light and coherence-scanning methods are also able to resolve the thickness of transparent films and buried interfaces.

Strengths

  • Full-field 3D topography, rather than a single point
  • Measures step heights, warpage, roughness and surface form
  • Dynamic interferometry is able to operate in vibration-prone environments, close to the process

Limitations

  • Thickness measurement is dependent on film transparency and contrast
  • Different instruments are suited to different scales

Typical applications. Optical surfaces, wafer flatness, MEMS, microlens arrays and package warpage.

Selecting a method

Begin by considering three questions:

  1. What information is required? Thickness alone, optical constants, or surface shape?
  2. How thin, and how complex, is the film stack?
  3. Where will measurement take place? In a laboratory, beside the line, or inline?

Many fabs employ more than one method: ellipsometry to build accurate material models, reflectometry for fast production mapping, and interferometry for surface and warpage measurement.
