How Laser Sample Preparation Speeds Up Semiconductor Failure Analysis

Failure analysis is frequently held up by sample preparation. Laser-based preparation removes bulk material rapidly, allowing FIB time to be reserved for the final, precise step.

When a device fails, engineers must be able to see the defect. In advanced packages, that defect may be buried millimetres deep beneath silicon, mould compound, solder and copper. Reaching it is often the slowest stage of failure analysis (FA).

The sample preparation bottleneck

The usual methods of reaching a buried target are:

  • Mechanical grinding and polishing. Low in equipment cost, but slow, operator-dependent and prone to smearing, cracking or deforming soft materials such as solder.
  • Focused ion beam (FIB). Highly precise, but gallium FIB removes material slowly. Removing large volumes can take many hours.
  • Plasma FIB (PFIB). Considerably faster than gallium FIB, but still costly per cubic millimetre where targets are deep or wide.

As packages become larger and increasingly three-dimensional, with stacked dies, interposers and hybrid-bonded interfaces, the volume of material to be removed continues to grow.

Where laser preparation fits

Laser ablation removes large volumes of material rapidly and without mechanical contact. It is used to:

  • Cut large-area package cross-sections
  • Expose flip-chip bumps, solder joints and solder balls without deformation
  • Remove MEMS caps or open up deep structures
  • Prepare targeted regions in TSVs, RDLs, interposers and hybrid-bonded stacks
  • Produce site-specific specimens for TEM, X-ray microscopy, atom-probe tomography and micromechanical testing

The principle is straightforward. Use the laser for bulk removal, and reserve FIB or PFIB time for the final few microns, where its precision is required.

A typical FA workflow

  1. Fault isolation. Non-destructive methods such as X-ray, CT and scanning acoustic microscopy are used to locate the suspect area.
  2. Laser bulk removal. Material is removed to a point close to the region of interest.
  3. Precise end-pointing. FIB or PFIB is used to polish the final surface.
  4. High-resolution analysis. SEM, TEM or other techniques are then used to reveal the defect and its cause.

Key considerations for a laser preparation system

  • Accurate positioning relative to the region of interest
  • Control of heat-affected zones to protect the target
  • Support for a range of sample formats, from wafers to boards
  • Straightforward transfer into your FIB, PFIB or SEM workflow
