What Is Hybrid Bonding? Wafer-to-Wafer vs Die-to-Wafer Explained

Hybrid bonding joins two chips through copper pads and dielectric simultaneously, without solder bumps. This article explains how it works, where it is used and what is required to achieve good yield.

Hybrid bonding is among the most important technologies in advanced packaging today. It enables chipmakers to stack dies at a far finer interconnect pitch than solder bumps permit. For this reason, it now underpins stacked image sensors, 3D cache chips and the roadmap for future high-bandwidth memory (HBM).

This guide explains what hybrid bonding is, how the wafer-to-wafer and die-to-wafer approaches differ, and the process control it requires.

What is hybrid bonding?

In a hybrid bond, two surfaces are joined in a single step through two materials simultaneously:

  • Dielectric to dielectric, typically silicon oxide or a comparable film, which bonds first at room temperature
  • Copper to copper, through pads embedded in the dielectric, which form a metallic connection during a subsequent anneal

As there is no solder bump, the pads can be considerably smaller and more closely spaced. Conventional micro-bumps are typically placed at pitches of tens of microns, whereas hybrid bonding operates at single-digit micron pitch and below. This allows many more connections per square millimetre, shorter signal paths and lower power per bit.

Wafer-to-wafer (W2W) vs die-to-wafer (D2W)

Wafer-to-wafer bonding joins two complete wafers. It offers high alignment accuracy and throughput, and is well established in stacked CMOS image sensors and certain memory designs. The trade-off is yield, since every die on one wafer is bonded to the die above it, whether good or bad.

Die-to-wafer bonding places individual known-good dies onto a target wafer. It is well suited to chiplets and heterogeneous integration, where dies originate from different processes or differ in size. It avoids stacking defective dies, but introduces additional challenges in die handling, cleanliness and placement.

Best forSame-size dies, high volumeChiplets, mixed dies, known-good die
AlignmentVery high, across the whole wafer at oncePer die, placement-dependent
Yield riskCompounds across both wafersLower, as only good dies are used
Main challengeFront-end yield matchingParticles, die handling, throughput

The hybrid bonding process, step by step

  1. CMP. Both surfaces must be extremely flat. Copper pads are polished so that they sit very slightly recessed, allowing them to expand into contact during the anneal.
  2. Cleaning. Any particle can create an unbonded void considerably larger than the particle itself.
  3. Plasma activation. Activation of the dielectric surface enables strong bonding at low temperature.
  4. Alignment and bonding. The two surfaces are aligned and brought into contact, with the dielectric bonding first.
  5. Anneal. Heat drives copper expansion and diffusion, forming the metal joint.
  6. Post-bond metrology. Inspection is carried out to check for voids, overlay error and bond strength.

Where hybrid bonding is used

  • Stacked image sensors, where pixel and logic wafers are bonded face to face
  • Logic-on-logic and cache stacking for high-performance processors
  • Chiplets that require dense die-to-die links
  • HBM, where it features on the roadmap for taller stacks. The research firm TrendForce reported that the major memory makers confirmed plans to use hybrid bonding for HBM5 20-high stacks, whilst HBM3e and HBM4 12-high stacks continue to use advanced MR-MUF and TC-NCF bonding.

What it takes to achieve yield

Hybrid bonding brings packaging closer to the discipline of the front-end fab. The principal yield factors are as follows:

  • Particle control. TrendForce also notes that particle control can increase unit costs, making cleanliness a matter of cost as well as quality.
  • Surface topography. Copper recess and dielectric flatness must be controlled at the nanometre level.
  • Overlay accuracy. Fine pitch leaves little margin for misalignment.
  • Bond inspection. Scanning acoustic microscopy and infrared inspection reveal voids, and failure analysis may require targeted cross-sections of the bonded interface.
