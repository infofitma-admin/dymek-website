Co-Packaged Optics (CPO) Explained: Why AI Data Centres Are Moving Optics onto the Package

Nvidia and Broadcom are now shipping co-packaged optics switches. This article explains what CPO is, why it reduces power consumption, and why it is demanding to manufacture.

AI clusters transfer very large volumes of data between accelerators and switches. Carrying that data over copper to pluggable optical transceivers at the front of a switch consumes considerable power. Co-packaged optics (CPO) shortens this path by placing the optics directly alongside the switch chip.

What is co-packaged optics?

In a conventional switch, the switch ASIC sends electrical signals across the circuit board to pluggable transceivers, each of which converts those signals into light.

In CPO, optical engines are placed in the same package as the switch ASIC. The electrical path is reduced from many centimetres to a few millimetres. This lowers both power per bit and latency, and frees space on the front panel.

Where CPO stands in 2026

A TrendForce report, covered by Electronics360 in July 2026, notes the following:

  • Nvidia and Broadcom have both entered volume production of CPO switches.
  • Broadcom's Bailly CPO switch reduces power consumption by up to 70% compared with conventional pluggable optics, and has been validated with hyperscalers such as Meta.
  • Nvidia's Spectrum-X CPO switch, developed with TSMC's COUPE packaging technology, offers up to 400 Tb/s of switching capacity.
  • TrendForce expects the main volume ramp of the CPO switch market to take place between 2027 and 2028.

The same report identifies three supply bottlenecks: optical engines, silicon photonics and advanced packaging. Each of these is, at its core, a manufacturing challenge.

Why CPO is difficult to manufacture

1. Fibre-to-chip coupling. Light must be coupled from a photonic chip into fibres with micron or sub-micron accuracy. Even a small misalignment results in significant optical loss. Active alignment, precision motion, and stable bonding and curing are therefore essential.

2. Heterogeneous integration. CPO combines electronic dies, photonic dies, lasers and optics within a single package. This calls for die-to-wafer bonding, thin-wafer handling and precise die placement, which is the same toolset used for chiplets and HBM.

3. Coupling structures. Grating couplers, edge couplers, micro-lenses and beam-shaping elements must be fabricated accurately at wafer scale, frequently by lithography or nanoimprint.

4. Known-good optical engines. As a CPO package is costly, each optical engine must be tested prior to assembly. This requires wafer- and chip-level optical and electrical probing of optical power, wavelength, beam behaviour and frequency response.

5. Inspection and failure analysis. Dense packages can conceal internal defects. X-ray, acoustic microscopy and targeted laser cross-sections help to identify root causes quickly.

What this means for manufacturers in Asia

A large share of the world's optical module and advanced packaging capacity is located in Asia. Companies moving from pluggable modules into CPO, optical I/O or silicon photonics require precision alignment, photonic packaging, metrology and test capability, together with engineering support close at hand.
